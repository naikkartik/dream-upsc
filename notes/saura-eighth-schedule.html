The Short Version

Executive Summary

There is no separate law for adding a language to the Eighth Schedule. It can only be done through a Constitutional Amendment under Article 368, passed by a special majority in both Houses of Parliament. No state ratification is required — which means the decisive lever is the Union Government's political will to introduce the Bill.

History shows the door is open but heavy. Every one of the 8 languages added since 1950 succeeded through a combination of: a sustained community movement, a distinct script and literature, an official state resolution, and — crucially — a moment of political leverage (a peace accord, a coalition need, or a mass agitation the Centre could not ignore).

The single most important finding

Speaker numbers alone do not decide inclusion. Sanskrit has ~25,000 speakers and is in; Bodo has 1.5 million and waited decades. What wins is organised, documented, politically-backed persistence. Saura's clearest precedent is Santhali — a tribal Munda language with its own script (Ol Chiki), added in 2003 after a decades-long movement. Saura (with the Sorang Sompeng script) can follow the same path.

Odisha has already done its part: the state Cabinet passed a resolution in June 2023 recommending Saura, and MPs continue to raise it in Parliament. The proposal now sits with the Centre. The community's job is to keep it from stalling — by building an unanswerable evidence dossier and sustained pressure.

Part 1

The Constitutional Mechanism

The Eighth Schedule flows from Articles 344(1) and 351 of the Constitution. Article 344 requires a Language Commission drawn from these languages; Article 351 directs the development of Hindi by drawing on them. Being listed brings real, tangible entitlements.

The legal route: Article 368

A language enters only by amending the Constitution itself. The steps in practice:

Demand from the community

Cultural bodies, writers and elected representatives formally raise the demand and build public momentum.

State government resolution

The State studies the language and passes a Cabinet/Assembly resolution recommending it to the Centre. Odisha did this for Saura in June 2023 →

Union Government examination

The Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) examines the case. If it decides to act, it drafts a Constitution Amendment Bill.

Passage by special majority

Both Houses pass it by a majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting. President assents; the language is added.

Two facts that work in our favour

1. No state ratification needed. The Eighth Schedule is not an entrenched provision, so it does not require ratification by half the state legislatures. Parliament alone can do it — a lower bar than most amendments.

2. It is routinely bipartisan. Every past inclusion passed with near-unanimous support, often in a single day. The blockage is never the vote — it is getting the Bill introduced in the first place.

What inclusion actually delivers

  • Sahitya Akademi recognition — the language is treated as a literary language by default, opening awards, grants and publication support.
  • Representation in Parliament — members may speak it, with translation provided.
  • Civil-service examinations — candidates may write UPSC and other exams in the language (a major youth benefit).
  • Development funds & institutions — a formal obligation on the State to develop, standardise and enrich the language.
  • Education — momentum for mother-tongue teaching material and school curricula.
Part 2

The Criteria Vacuum — the real reason things stall

Here is the paradox at the heart of the whole issue: there are no fixed, official criteria for what qualifies a language for inclusion. The government has admitted this in Parliament as recently as 2021.

Two committees were set up specifically to create objective criteria — and both failed to produce a usable formula:

Pahwa Committee (1996)

Set up under Ashok Pahwa, Secretary (Official Language), MHA, to evolve inclusion criteria. Did not produce final, adopted criteria.

Sitakant Mahapatra Committee (2003–04)

Tasked again with fixing objective criteria. It grappled with the problem but ultimately recommended status quo, unable to design standards that would avoid politically-motivated claims and an endless cascade of new demands.

Since the 2003 additions, no new language has been added — a deliberate policy of restraint while 38+ demands pile up.

Why this matters for our strategy

Because there are no fixed criteria, inclusion is ultimately a political and executive decision, not a checklist you can "pass." That is both the frustration and the opportunity: a well-organised community that creates political salience — as the Bodos, Gorkhas and Santhals did — can move its case to the front of the queue. Our task is to make Saura impossible to ignore.

Part 3

How Every Added Language Got In

Since the original 14 languages of 1950, exactly 8 more have been added across three amendments. Each is a case study in what works.

Sindhi — 21st Amendment, 1967 ~20 years

After Partition, Sindhi-speakers became refugees scattered across Maharashtra, Gujarat, MP and Rajasthan — a large linguistic community with no home state. Persistent demands and a recommendation by the Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities led the Government to announce inclusion in November 1966; the Bill was introduced, passed by both Houses in April 1967, and assented on 10 April 1967.

Lesson for Saura: a language does not need a state or a territory to qualify. A distinct, substantial community and organised advocacy is enough.

Konkani, Manipuri (Meitei) & Nepali — 71st Amendment, 1992

Nepali is the great endurance story: the first formal demand was made on 18 January 1956 (initially rejected as a "foreign language"), followed by a 36-year Bhasa Andolan led by bodies like the Akhil Bharatiya Nepali Bhasa Samiti (founded 1972), until victory on 20 August 1992 — now celebrated annually as Nepali Bhasa Manyata Diwas.

Konkani rode the wave of Goa's statehood (1987) and prior Sahitya Akademi recognition (1975). Manipuri/Meitei was a major regional language of a full state. All three were bundled into one amendment, passed in a single day.

Lesson for Saura: sustained movements win, even over decades — and bundling with allied language demands adds weight. (Saura is already bundled with Ho, Mundari, Bhumij and Kui in Odisha's push.)

Bodo, Dogri, Maithili & Santhali — 92nd Amendment, 2003

This is the most instructive amendment for us. The Bill was introduced by Deputy PM L.K. Advani in August 2003 originally for Bodo alone, then expanded during debate to add Dogri, Maithili and Santhali; passed by Rajya Sabha on 23 December 2003.

  • Bodo — driven by the Bodo Accord (Memorandum of Settlement). The Centre had committed to consider Eighth Schedule inclusion as part of a peace settlement. Political leverage was the decisive factor.
  • Maithili — a decades-long literary movement (Maithili Sahitya Sansthan active since the 1960s) finally rewarded.
  • Santhali — the closest parallel to Saura: a tribal Munda language with its own script (Ol Chiki, created 1925 by Raghunath Murmu), backed by a long movement and boosted by the creation of Jharkhand (2000). Recognition then drove Ol Chiki into school curricula and even a Santhali edition of the Constitution.

Santhali is our template

Munda language family ✓ · own indigenous script ✓ · tribal community ✓ · long documented movement ✓. Saura shares every one of these traits. What Santhali added was scale and political organisation — that is precisely the gap Saura must close.

Two honest reads of this table

The challenge: Saura's ~4.1 lakh speakers would make it one of the smallest scheduled languages — smaller than Bodo. Rival Odisha demands (Ho, Kui, Mundari) actually have larger speaker bases, so Saura cannot win on numbers alone.

The opening: Numbers are clearly not the deciding factor — Sanskrit sits in the Schedule with ~25,000 speakers on the strength of heritage. Saura's case must lean on its ancient lineage, distinct Unicode-encoded script, PVTG status, and cultural uniqueness — exactly the grounds on which Santhali and Sanskrit succeeded.

Part 6

What Made the Winners Win — 6 patterns

1 · A sustained, organised movement

Every success ran for 2–4 decades through dedicated bodies (Nepali Bhasa Samiti, Maithili Sahitya Sansthan). A standing organisation that outlives individual leaders is essential.

2 · A distinct script & documented literature

Santhali's Ol Chiki was decisive proof of a self-standing language. Saura has Sorang Sompeng (1936, Unicode 2012) — this must be foregrounded and its literature expanded.

3 · An official state resolution

The State endorsing the demand to the Centre is a near-mandatory step. Saura already has this (2023) — a major advantage that must be actively followed up, not left to gather dust.

4 · Political leverage / a "moment"

Bodo rode a peace accord; Konkani rode statehood; Nepali rode mass agitation. Saura should tie its demand to tribal-welfare politics, PVTG protection, and electoral salience in Odisha/Andhra.

5 · Strength in numbers via bundling

The 2003 amendment added four languages at once. Odisha's grouping of Saura with Ho, Mundari, Bhumij and Kui creates a bloc the Centre can address in one Bill.

6 · Champions in Parliament

Someone must keep raising it in the House. MPs are already doing so — the community must keep feeding them evidence and keep the pressure continuous.

Part 8

Our Action Roadmap

A phased plan the community can start on immediately, benchmarked against how Santhali and Bodo succeeded.

Phase 1 — Organise & unify (0–6 months)

Form a single apex body

An "All-India Saura Language Council" uniting Sora associations across Odisha and Andhra — one recognised voice that outlives individuals, like the Nepali Bhasa Samiti.

Bring in allies

Coordinate formally with the Ho, Mundari, Bhumij and Kui campaigns so the Odisha bloc speaks together — more weight in one Bill.

Phase 2 — Build the evidence dossier (6–18 months)

Document the language

Commission/compile a Saura dictionary, grammar, and a corpus of literature in Sorang Sompeng. This is the proof of a "self-standing language" that won Santhali its case.

Strengthen the script

Promote Sorang Sompeng in schools, printing, and digital fonts/keyboards (it's already in Unicode). Living daily use is powerful evidence.

Assemble the data pack

Speaker statistics, geographic spread, PVTG status, UNESCO endangerment listing, cultural heritage — a single authoritative memorandum.

Phase 3 — Petition the right doors (12–24 months, parallel)

Target the deciders

Memoranda to the Union Ministry of Home Affairs (which owns the process) and the Ministry of Culture / Sahitya Akademi. Formally follow up Odisha's 2023 resolution — ask the state to press the Centre.

Seek interim recognition

Push for Saura in state school curricula, a state language academy, and Sahitya Akademi engagement — builds the record even before the Schedule.

Phase 4 — Sustain political & public pressure (ongoing)

Keep MPs supplied and active

Ensure Saura is raised in every session; brief MPs across parties with fresh evidence. Make cross-party support visible.

Create a "moment"

An annual Saura Language Day, cultural festivals, media stories, and tying the demand to tribal-welfare and PVTG-protection politics to generate the salience that moved Bodo and Nepali.

The realistic expectation

Based on every precedent, this is a campaign measured in years, not months. But the two hardest assets — a distinct script and a state resolution — Saura already has. With disciplined organisation and documentation, the Sora community is genuinely well-positioned to be next through the door whenever the Centre reopens it.
